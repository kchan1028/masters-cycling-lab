Masters Cycling Lab

Masters Cycling Lab is a research app that runs on your own computer. You load cycling research papers as PDFs, ask a question in plain English, and get a short answer in which every claim links to the passage and PDF page it came from.

The short version

It is good at telling you which study says what, and it lets you check every answer against the source. It is still weak at finding the exact paragraph that holds the answer, and it is a reading tool, not a coach.

  1. You can check every answer.Each claim cites a numbered passage. Click it and the PDF opens at that page.
  2. It finds the right paper far more often than the right paragraph.About three in four relevant papers show up in the top ten results. The passage with the answer shows up less than half the time.
  3. It is a one-person research tool.No login, no training plans, no medical advice. It runs on 127.0.0.1 and nowhere else.

How does Masters Cycling Lab answer a question?

Masters Cycling Lab finds the ten passages in your papers that are closest in meaning to your question, sends only those ten to a language model, and keeps the answer only if every claim cites one of them.

This approach is called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG: look things up first, then write the answer from what you found. The model is asked to answer from those passages only, and the app rejects any citation to a passage it was not shown.

When there is nothing to cite. If search returns no passages, the app says there is not enough evidence and never calls the model. Otherwise the model decides, and it can also reply that the passages do not answer the question.

How well does it work?

On 20 research papers and 74 test questions, Masters Cycling Lab put about three in four relevant papers in its top ten results, but the passage holding the answer was there less than half the time.

The test questions come with hand-checked answers: which papers are relevant and the exact sentences, with page numbers, that answer them. A person reviewed all 74 and changed none. 37 practice questions were used while tuning; 18 fresh ones were held back and run once.

Are the answers right?

In the first seven answers a person checked, all 19 claims were supported by the passages they cited. That is a small, easy sample, and the harder questions have not run yet.

Answers were generated with Google's Gemini 3.6 Flash on a free tier, which allows about a dozen answers a day. That is why there are so few so far. Each answer took a median of 25 seconds to write; the search behind it took about 25 milliseconds.

  • With 10 passages, all 7 questions were answered and every answer cited the paper graded most relevant.
  • With the earlier setting of 6 passages, 2 of 15 answerable questions came back as "not enough evidence". In both cases the model had the right paper but not the paragraph with the finding. With 10 passages, the one retested so far (q010) was answered and cited the right page.
  • The six questions that should get "not enough evidence" or "out of scope" (bike buying, FTP zones and beetroot among them) have not reached the model yet. Whether it declines them properly is still unknown.

Why does it sometimes miss the answer?

Usually because the passage with the answer never reaches the model. With 10 passages, only 20 of the 37 answerable practice questions had any answer passage among those sent.

Several fixes were tried: mixing in keyword search, capping passages per paper, smaller passages, and three rerankers that re-score the top results. Some helped on the practice questions. None beat plain meaning-based search on the fresh ones, so none is used for answers. The next step is measuring answers end to end rather than running another ranking experiment.

What research is in the library?

The test library is 20 open-access papers on masters and older cyclists, training and physiology: 259 pages cut into 1,269 passages.

All 20 are published under CC BY and downloaded from PubMed Central's open-access collection, each checked against a recorded SHA-256 fingerprint. You can add your own PDFs from the Papers page; they stay on your machine and are never stored in git.

What can't it do?

  • It quotes studies. It does not write training plans or give medical guidance.
  • It has no login and is meant for one person. Keep it on 127.0.0.1 and don't expose it to a network.
  • It can't read scanned PDFs, because there is no OCR. Tables lose their layout, figures are skipped, and two-column pages can mix their lines.
  • A sentence that crosses a page break gets split in two, because passages stop at the page edge.
  • Answers need a language model you choose, at any OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Your question and the ten passages are sent to it; API keys stay on the server. Search and the PDF viewer work without one.

What is it built with?

Five pieces, started together with Docker Compose. There is no separate vector database and no RAG framework.

PieceWhat it does
Web app (React, served by nginx)Ask a question, Papers, Search details and Search scores pages
API (Python, FastAPI)Search, answers, citations and the PDF viewer
Worker (Python)Reads and indexes new PDFs in the background, with retries
PostgreSQL with pgvectorStores pages, passages, their numbers, answers and test runs
Language model (your choice)Any OpenAI-compatible endpoint; called only to write answers

PDF reading uses PyMuPDF, which is licensed AGPL-3.0. That matters if you distribute the app or run it for other people. The project itself has no licence yet.

What happens next?

Finish the answer run on a provider with a higher daily limit, including the six questions it should decline, and rate every claim the way the first 19 were rated. Then work on picking the right paragraph inside the right paper, which is where most misses start.

To run it yourself, follow the README. The short path, after copying .env.example to .env and setting a database password:

make bootstrap
make up
make migrate
make download-model
make corpus-download
make corpus-ingest

Then open http://127.0.0.1:8080.
